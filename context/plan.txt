  Executive summary
  Build a routing engine, not a price predictor. After a VIPER scan, the system ranks the
  channels a vehicle could go to (ACV dealer wholesale, Copart salvage, Copart's fleet and
  bank lane, a recycler, a direct buyer) by expected net proceeds, probability the sale
  completes, time to cash and post-sale risk, then names the buyer segment most likely to
  pay. A good buyer is the one with the highest expected net to the seller after fees,
  transport and unwind risk, with a completion probability above a floor, not the highest
  bidder.

  Three facts shape everything below:

         ACV and Copart are now one company. Copart completed its $1.9 billion, $10.50-per-
         share acquisition of ACV on 16 Oct 2026; ACV runs as an independent subsidiary.
         Copart describes the combination as a remarketing platform spanning trade-ins,
         wholesale and salvage. The channel question this doc answers is now an internal
         problem for them, which is both the biggest opportunity and the biggest platform risk.
         The volumes are large and already segmented. Copart sold more than 4 million units
         in its last fiscal year, 81% of them from insurers. ACV sold 829,276 units in 2025
         through 14,905 sellers and 22,062 buyers. Copart's fleet, bank and rental lane (Blue
         Car) grew about 20% in its latest quarter, which is where mis-routed vehicles most
         likely sit today.
         We cannot get their data before a prototype exists, and we cannot price repairs
         precisely. So the mechanism is built on proxy data (public Copart results, VIN decode,
         price indexes, our own scans), synthetic outcomes and a backtest harness, with a
         published data contract so their data plugs in later. The output is a ranked
         recommendation with a confidence band and a short list of what to verify, never a
         single number.

  The question, restated
  The decision is "where does this vehicle earn the most for its seller, net and risk-adjusted,
  and who is the buyer there", taken once per vehicle right after the scan. VIPER and its
  predecessors already produce the condition facts; what nobody produces is the
  comparison across channels that now sit inside one company.

  Why "predict the price" is the wrong framing:

         A price point will be wrong often, and the first wrong number costs you the seller's
         trust. A ranking only has to get the order right, and it can say when two channels are
         too close to call.
         Repair cost cannot be estimated precisely from photos (frame, mechanical and flood
         damage are invisible), so any channel that depends on a repair estimate must carry a
         wide band. The ranking can still be confident when the gap between channels is larger
         than the band.
         Routing is a treatment-effect problem. For each past vehicle you only observe the
         channel it went to, never the counterfactual. The mechanism therefore has to be built
         around explicit assumptions, simulation and controlled experiments, not a single
         supervised model.

  What the engine must answer for every scanned vehicle:

     1. Which channels are legally and contractually available (title type, state rules, seller
        agreements)?
     2. For each available channel, what is the distribution of net proceeds to the seller, the
        probability the sale completes without unwind, and the days to cash?
     3. Which buyer segment inside the winning channel is the likely payer (dealer by region
        and inventory gap, rebuilder, dismantler, exporter, fleet buyer), and how reliable is
        that segment?
     4. How confident is the ranking, and which single piece of missing information would
        change it?
     5. What should a human verify before the vehicle is assigned?

  What the volumes say about demand:

         Copart's CEO said the ACV combination could broaden access to franchise dealers
         and higher-end trade-ins while supporting demand for lightly damaged insurance
         vehicles and international sales. That is the routing problem stated by the buyer:
         some insurance cars belong in the dealer channel, and some trade-ins belong with
         Copart's international buyers.
         Buyers already cross channels. Over the past three years a majority of the 30,000-
         plus members who entered Copart through non-insurance vehicles bid on an
         insurance vehicle within 90 days, so a buyer-fit model has a real population to learn
         from.
         Rental sellers are choosing repair over sale more often (Q3 FY2026 call), which means
         the fix-versus-sell decision is live and currently made with the seller's own rules.
         Total-loss frequency hit 23.3% in Q2 FY2026 (Nov 2025 to Jan 2026), a record for that
         quarter, so the supply of damaged-but-sellable vehicles keeps rising while U.S. unit
         volume fell 5.7% in Q4 FY2026. Routing that improves revenue per unit is worth more
         to Copart when units are flat.

  dealers, dismantlers, rebuilders, exporters and the public (Copart release, 16 Oct 2026).

         The condition layer is solved by the incumbents. VIPER captures 360-degree exterior
         imagery, undercarriage images, automatic damage detection and tire tread in under

  Definition of a good buyer
  A good buyer for a given vehicle is the buyer segment whose expected net to the seller is
  highest after fees, transport, holding time and post-sale risk, with a completion
  probability above a floor the seller sets. The highest bid is not the test; a bid that is later
  arbitrated, unwound or unpaid is worth less than a lower bid that closes.

  The definition works at two levels. At the channel level, an auction lane is a mix of buyer
  segments, so choosing the lane is choosing the mix; the engine picks the lane whose
  buyer mix maximizes expected net for this vehicle. At the buyer level, for direct offers or
  invitation-only sales, the same scorecard ranks named buyers.

     \mathrm{Net}_{c} = \mathbb{E}[\mathrm{Price}_{c}] - \mathrm{Fees}_{c} -
     \mathrm{Transport}_{c} - \mathrm{Hold}_{c}(d) - P_{c}
     (\mathrm{dispute})\,\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{credit}] - P_{c}
     (\mathrm{unwind})\,\mathbb{E}[\mathrm{relist\ loss}]


  where c is a channel (or a buyer segment inside it), d is expected days to cash, and every
  term carries a range, not a point.

  Decision rules on top of the score:

         Rank channels by expected net; show the top two with their bands. If the bands
         overlap by more than a threshold (start at 15% of the lower band), declare "too close
         to call" and name the one check that would separate them.

         Apply the seller's preference as weights: a fleet remarketer may trade $200 of
         expected net for ten fewer days to cash; a dealer may refuse any channel with
         arbitration exposure above a limit.
         Exclude channels the title or state law forbids before scoring (non-repairable titles
         cannot be rebuilt or registered in Texas and must go to a licensed wrecker in Nevada).
         Route high-dispute-risk vehicles (hidden mechanical or frame unknowns) to as-is
         channels unless the price gain in a guaranteed channel exceeds the expected dispute
         cost.


  Platform requirements
  The platform takes a scanned vehicle and returns a ranked channel recommendation with
  a buyer segment, a net-proceeds band, a confidence level and a verification list, within 60
  seconds of the scan, and records what happened afterwards so the next
  recommendation is better.

  Inputs

     1. VIPER (or any inspection) payload: VIN, 360 exterior images, undercarriage images,
         damage list (part, type, severity, image reference), tire tread, timestamps. When
         VIPER is absent, the same schema is filled from Underscope's own photo flow
         (exterior, interior, engine bay, underbody).
     2. Non-visual facts the scan cannot give: odometer, title brand and state, keys, runs-and-
        drives, engine start clip (ACV records 30 seconds of engine audio through startup,
        idle, two revs and shutoff for its reports), dash warning lights.
     3. Seller profile: seller type, location, contracts and lanes available to them, preference
         weights (price vs speed vs risk), repair capability.
     4. Market data feeds: clean values, salvage comps, parts prices, fee schedules, transport
        rates.

  Outputs

     1. Ranked list of available channels with expected net (P10, P50, P90), days to cash,
        completion probability and dispute risk.
     2. Buyer segment most likely to pay in the winning channel, with the reason in one
        sentence.
     3. Confidence: clear winner, close call, or insufficient information, plus the single check
        that would most change the ranking.

     4. Evidence pack: the images, measurements and comparables behind the numbers, so a
        human or a buyer can audit it.
     5. A decision record: inputs, model versions, recommendation, who approved it, what
        the seller chose, and the realized outcome when known.

  Functional requirements

         Eligibility filter runs first: title brand, state rules, seller contract, export restrictions;
         excluded channels are shown as excluded with the reason.
         Every channel estimate is a distribution from comparables plus model adjustment,
         never a single predicted price.
         Repair-cost estimates carry an explicit band and a list of invisible-risk items (frame,
         mechanical, flood) that widen it.
         Human review is routed by rule: close calls, high-value vehicles, low-confidence vision
         output, and a random blind sample, 3% at the start and raised as bands calibrate,
         reviewed without seeing the model's answer, to measure what the model misses.
         Outcome capture is mandatory: the system cannot close a decision record without a
         realized channel and price or an explicit "unknown".
         Explanations are written for a used-car manager, not a data scientist: three reasons,
         two risks, one next step.
         Seller preferences change the ranking in real time and are stored with the decision
         record.

  Non-functional requirements

         Latency: ranking in under 60 seconds after the scan; market data cached and
         refreshed daily.
         Scale: 1,000 vehicles a month in the pilot, designed for 100,000 a month (Copart's U.S.
         volume is several hundred thousand a month).
         Auditability: every number traceable to its inputs and model version for 7 years,
         matching the retention sellers' finance teams expect.
         Data boundaries: seller data never trains a model used for another seller's competitor
         without contract permission; buyer identities stay anonymized outside the platform
         owner.
         Portability: the engine runs as a service behind an API; Copart/ACV can host it inside
         their environment with their data never leaving.
         Model governance: a model cannot be promoted without passing the backtest
         harness in section 11 on held-out vehicles.

  Underlying logic and architecture

  estimators. No stage is a single black-box prediction, and every stage can be replaced

  channel-routing engine · 7 stages, 4 estimators, 1 feedback loop

  The scan and the seller's facts pass a rules-only filter, become a part-level condition
  graph, are priced by four independent estimators fed by swappable data adapters, ranked
  by one policy, reviewed by rule, delivered with evidence, and closed with a mandatory
  outcome that recalibrates the estimators.

  How the policy ranks:



                                                                                          Page 17 of 27
Underscope: Channel-Routing Intelligence — Research Plan, Requirements and Architecture




     1. Draw 2,000 samples from each channel's net distribution (price band, days, dispute
         and unwind probabilities).
     2. Compute the share of samples in which each channel wins; the recommended
        channel is the one with the highest share, not the highest mean.
     3. If the top two shares are within the close-call threshold, compute which single input
        (frame check, engine clip, title confirmation) would most reduce the overlap; that
        becomes the verification item.
     4. Apply seller weights (price, speed, risk) as a utility over the samples, then re-rank.
     5. Name the buyer segment with the highest expected contribution inside the winning
        channel, with one reason.

  Interfaces, so the engine can live inside Copart/ACV later:

          POST /route takes the scan payload, facts and seller profile; returns the ranking,
         bands, buyer segment, confidence and verification list.
         Market adapters are plug-ins behind one schema ( comps , values , parts , fees ,
          transport ); the prototype ships proxy adapters, the pilot swaps in theirs.

          POST /outcome closes a decision record; a record cannot close without a realized
         channel and price or an explicit unknown.
         Every response carries model versions and an evidence pack id for audit.

  Simulation, used for the policy and never as a result:

         Fit a distribution per channel to the proxies, then simulate 10,000 vehicles with known
         "true" outcomes in every channel. This is the only place the counterfactual exists, so it
         is where the ranking policy, the close-call threshold and the review router are tuned.
         Run sensitivity on every parameter. The ones that flip the most decisions (expected:
         dispute cost in the guaranteed channel, rebuilt-title discount, days to cash for parts)
         become the first measurements of the pilot.
         Publish regret: how much net the policy gives up versus a perfect oracle, so the pilot
         has a number to beat.

  The evaluation harness (a model is promoted only if it passes):




                                                                                                                    Page 19 of 27
Underscope: Channel-Routing Intelligence — Research Plan, Requirements and Architecture




     1. Condition: dollar-weighted recall of damage against blind human labels on our own
         scans; missing a $2,000 part counts twenty times a $100 scratch.
     2. Price bands: on held-out Copart lots, the P10 to P90 band must cover 80% of realized
        prices and be no wider than a fixed-width baseline.
     3. Ranking: on lots where the realized channel is known, the probability-of-winning
        estimate must be calibrated (when the engine says 70%, it is right about 70% of the
        time).
     4. Plug-in: swapping an adapter for a partner dataset requires zero code changes and a
        re-run of tests 1 to 3.
     5. Shadow mode at the pilot: the engine recommends silently next to the current lane
        rules; agreement rate, and lift measured only on a randomized 10% slice where the
        engine's choice is followed.

  What we need from them to switch out of proxy mode is the contract in section 7; what
  we need from no one is everything above.


  Operational costs
  Running the engine costs about $2 to $5 per vehicle at pilot volume and under $1 per
  vehicle at scale, and more than 60% of the scaled cost is human review, not AI. The design
  lever is therefore the review router, and the breakeven bar is low: at 100,000 vehicles a
  month the engine pays for itself if it adds about $0.70 of net per vehicle scanned, for
  example $14 more on one vehicle in twenty.

  ready for a shadow-mode pilot inside one partner's workflow in about 90 days if scope
  narrows to one seller segment (fleet, rental or finance units with damage) and one region.

  90-day plan and the questions for Copart/ACV
  The goal of the 90 days is one sentence we can say to Copart with evidence: "we built the
  routing mechanism, it is calibrated on the salvage side, and here is the test that measures
  lift once your data is plugged in". The plan has three phases, each with a gate that can stop
  the project.




  90-day plan · 3 phases, 3 gates

  Phase 1 builds the mechanism on proxies, Phase 2 adds partner evidence and calibration,
  and Phase 3 runs it silently beside a partner's current rules; each gate must pass before
  the next phase starts.

  Pilot design to propose at Gate 2:

         Metric: realized net proceeds per contestable vehicle, engine-routed versus rule-
         routed, with days to cash and dispute rate as secondary measures.
         Sample: about 1,600 vehicles per arm if the expected lift is $150 against a $1,500
         spread in net (80% power, 5% significance); recompute once the real spread is
         known.
         Duration: 30 days in shadow mode, then 30 days with a randomized 10% slice following
         the engine.
         Scope: one lane (fleet, rental or finance units with damage), one region, one objective
         (seller net), reported weekly.

  Questions to put to Copart/ACV, in the order to ask them:

     1. Who owns the "right vehicle to the right buyer through the right channel" capability
        after the merger, and what have they already decided to build?
     2. What share of Blue Car and dealer consignments do they see as contestable between
         salvage, wholesale and ACV lanes?



                                                                                              Page 25 of 27
Underscope: Channel-Routing Intelligence — Research Plan, Requirements and Architecture




     3. Would they run a 60-day shadow-mode pilot on one lane under NDA, with the data
         contract in section 7 as the input?
     4. Can ACV share the VIPER payload schema and a sample of anonymized scans?
     5. What aggregate arbitration, unwind and days-to-cash figures by buyer segment can
        they share to replace our proxies?
     6. Would they prefer to license the engine, host it, or acquire it, and what evidence
         would move them from pilot to production?

  What we want to achieve, stated so it can fail: a calibrated engine, a measured contestable

  production. If Copart/ACV decline, the same engine and evidence go to fleet and rental
  remarketers, whose volume into Copart grew about 20% in the latest quarter.
